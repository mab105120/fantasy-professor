Method & caveats

  • Grades are a blend, because one 2026 game is not a sample. Offense score = 60% 2025 points per game + 20% Week 1 2026 points + 20% Week 1 yards per play, each standardized across the 32 teams (a z-score: how many standard deviations above or below the league average). Defense is the same with points allowed and yards per play allowed. Teams are then ranked 1–32 on each and lettered by rank (A = top 3, B = 4–9, C = 10–21, D = 22–29, F = bottom 3, with +/− within bands).
  • What the prior misses: offseason change. A team with a new quarterback or coordinator (Seattle under Drew Lock this week, Atlanta with Cooper Rush, Denver in Nix's second year) is graded partly on a 2025 that may not describe it. Where the Week 1 number disagrees hard with the 2025 number — Chicago's 59-point W1 on a 25.9 ppg prior, Carolina's 59 allowed — the grade is already moving; by Week 4 the current-season weight should rise to 50%+.
  • Yards per play is the most stable one-game stat there is (it's not distorted by a single turnover or a short field the way points are), which is why it gets equal weight with points in the Week 1 component.
  • Unit-vs-unit reads are environment, not projections. A "favorable" read raises a player's ceiling and floor a little; it doesn't override role or health. Olave in a bad offense against a middling defense is still a 13-target receiver.
  • Projections are medians: team totals land within ~25 points of the projected sum about two-thirds of the time. Lines move; ESPN's table and reporters differ by a point on three games.
  • Player-to-game mapping and league projections are generated from data/week_stats.json (Week 2, in progress, synced 21:04 CT). Grades are computed from ESPN's public box-score and standings data pulled at ~23:00 CT.
